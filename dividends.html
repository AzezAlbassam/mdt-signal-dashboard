The dividend snowball

What Buffett’s Coca-Cola position actually pays him, the arithmetic that produced it, and a live screen of small companies that fit the same profile today — before they get big.

Start with the real numbers

The story going around is roughly right in shape and badly wrong in size. Here is what Berkshire Hathaway’s Coca-Cola stake actually looks like, using Coca-Cola’s live dividend and share price:

So the position pays about $210 million a quarter, not $2 million — and the cost was $1.3 billion, not $800,000. The holding period is 38 years, not 50. Coca-Cola has raised its dividend for 64 straight years, not 26.

The part of the story that is true is the part that matters: Berkshire has collected roughly $11 billion in cash dividends on a $1.3 billion cost — it got its money back about eight times over without selling anything — and still owns a stake worth around $35 billion. That is the snowball. It just took four decades.

The same trade at a normal size

A billion dollars is impossible to feel. Buffett’s cost works out to $3.25 a share, so the whole thing scales down cleanly — the mechanism is identical at every size, only the zeros change. Put $14,000 in at his price and you would have bought 4,311 shares:

And that is before reinvesting anything. Those figures are price appreciation plus the current dividend on the original shares. Take every dividend along the way and buy more shares with it, and the end number moves into seven figures — reinvestment is worth several times the capital gain on its own over a period this long.

That is the whole argument for doing this early and leaving it alone. The Nanalyze video this dashboard started from puts the reinvested total at $1.85 million on a $14,000 stake. Exact reinvested totals depend on the entry date and where every dividend got bought back in, so treat the seven-figure end point as the shape of the result rather than a figure to check to the dollar — the four numbers above are the ones that are precisely verifiable.

The engine, in one line

Every dividend-growth story reduces to the same expression. There is no other mechanism:

yield on cost after t years = y0 × (1 + g)t y0 = the yield you buy at  ·  g = annual dividend growth  ·  t = years you hold

For Coca-Cola: Berkshire bought at roughly a 3.2% yield and the dividend compounded at about 8.5% a year for 37 years. That turns 3.2% into 64.7%. Nothing exotic happened — a mid-single-digit starting yield met decades of steady raises.

The formula also tells you where the leverage is. Doubling your starting yield doubles your end result. Adding three points to g multiplies it by three or four. Growth compounds; yield does not. That is why a 1.8% yield growing at 14% beats a 5% yield growing at 3% — and it beats it decisively, in year eleven:

5.0% yield × 3% growth, after 15 years
61.4%

The bucket list — ten rules

These are written as tests, not opinions. A company either passes or it does not, and the screen below applies the measurable ones automatically.

  1. Small enough that growth still moves the needle

    Coca-Cola is a $378 billion company. It cannot grow its dividend at 10% for twenty more years, because it would have to become the largest company on earth to fund it. The whole game is buying the compounding before the market caps it out.

    market cap < $15B · ideally < $5B
  2. A starting yield in the usable band

    Below about 1% the snowball starts too small to matter inside a human lifetime. Above about 5% in a small company, the market is usually telling you the dividend is at risk or the business has stopped growing. The sweet spot is unglamorous.

    1.5% ≤ yield ≤ 4.0%
  3. Dividend growth above 8% a year

    This is the single highest-weighted input, because it is the one that compounds. Below 6% you are roughly tracking inflation and the snowball never really forms. Above 12% sustained is where twenty-year outcomes get interesting.

    5-year dividend CAGR ≥ 8% · prefer ≥ 12%
  4. A payout ratio with room left in it

    The most underrated rule. A company paying out 25% of free cash flow can double its dividend without growing profits at all, just by moving to 50%. A company already at 80% can only raise as fast as it grows. Low payout is stored future growth.

    payout 20–60% of free cash flow
  5. A streak long enough to prove policy, short enough to still be fast

    Five years tells you management means it. But the 50- and 60-year streaks are mostly mature businesses raising 3% a year to protect the record. Counter-intuitively, the best hunting is 10 to 25 years — the policy is established and the growth is still real.

    10 ≤ streak ≤ 30 years
  6. Earnings growing at least as fast as the dividend

    If the dividend grows 12% while free cash flow grows 4%, the payout ratio is climbing and the raises are being borrowed from the future. Check that the payout ratio today is not much higher than it was five years ago.

    FCF growth ≥ dividend growth, over 5 years
  7. High return on invested capital

    A business earning 20% on capital can fund its own growth and still hand you cash. A business earning 7% has to choose. This is what separates a compounder from a company that merely pays a dividend.

    ROIC > 12% · prefer > 15%
  8. A balance sheet that is not competing with you

    Debt gets paid before shareholders do. In the downturn that will happen at some point in your twenty years, a leveraged company cuts the dividend and an unleveraged one raises it.

    net debt / EBITDA < 2.5×
  9. One sentence on why competitors cannot take the business

    Switching costs, a regulated monopoly, franchise royalties, distribution density, a brand people ask for by name. If you cannot write the sentence, you do not have a twenty-year holding — you have a stock.

    write it down · if you cannot, skip it
  10. Management that treats the dividend as a promise

    Did it survive 2008 and 2020 without a cut? Do insiders own meaningful stock? Is there a stated policy? A company that cut in 2020 and quietly restarted later has told you what it does under pressure.

    no cut in the last two recessions

The disqualifiers

  • A yield above 7% on a small company. That is not a bargain, it is a forecast. The market is pricing the cut before it is announced.
  • A trailing yield inflated by a special dividend. Four names in the screen below do this — their headline yield is two to four times their real, repeatable one. The screen flags them and scores them on the regular dividend only.
  • A dividend funded by debt or asset sales rather than by free cash flow. Compare dividends paid against free cash flow in the cash-flow statement, not against earnings.
  • Payout above 90% of free cash flow. There is no room left for a raise and no cushion for a bad year.
  • Steady share issuance. If the share count climbs every year, part of your dividend growth is being handed to new shareholders.
  • Fixed payouts on cyclical earnings — homebuilders, shipping, commodity chemicals. The streak looks fine right up until the cycle turns, which it will do at least twice in twenty years.

Where the numbers come from

Three different kinds of number share this table, and they are not equally reliable:
· Price, market cap and the trailing dividend are live from a market data feed, refreshed on the date in the line above.
· Growth, payout and streak marked filed are computed from the company’s own 10-K filings via SEC EDGAR’s XBRL data. For each company one pass derives the annual regular dividend per share — choosing between the filed annual figure, the four quarterly rates, and dividends paid divided by shares; adjusting the years that were filed on a pre-split share basis; separating special dividends from the regular series — and computes free cash flow, payout, ROIC and leverage from the cash-flow and balance-sheet tags. Two further passes then try to refute that work, one recomputing every figure from the raw filings, one checking it against the live price, share count and what the business plausibly is. A company whose record is blocked by either reviewer is recomputed before it appears here.
· Anything marked est is my earlier research estimate, not yet replaced by filed data. Treat those as a starting point for your own check, not as a figure to act on.

XBRL begins around 2009, so a streak longer than the filing window cannot be confirmed from filings alone; where the research figure is longer and plausible, the table shows it and the company’s own page says which years the data actually confirms.


The score is out of 100, from five components, all applied to the regular dividend (special dividends are excluded):
· Entry yield, 20 pts — full marks in the 1.5–4.0% band, falling away above and below.
· Dividend growth, 30 pts — the heaviest weight, because it is the term that compounds. Full marks in the 12–20% band; above 20% scores slightly lower, because a dividend growing that fast is usually young, small-based and about to decelerate.
· Payout runway, 20 pts — full marks at or below 25% of free cash flow, zero above 85%.
· Size, 15 pts — full marks under $2B, 2 points above $15B.
· Track record, 15 pts — full marks for a 10–30 year streak; less for shorter (unproven) and for 50+ (mature, slow).

YoC in 20y is the regular yield compounded forward — the Coca-Cola calculation applied to each name. It does not hold the current growth rate flat for twenty years, because no company does that. Growth runs at the recent rate for five years, then fades linearly to a 5% terminal rate by year twenty. Hover any figure in that column to see what it would have been on a flat extrapolation; for the fastest growers the difference is three- or four-fold, and that gap is the single biggest source of error in this kind of analysis.
